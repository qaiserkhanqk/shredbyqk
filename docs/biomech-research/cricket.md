# Cricket biomechanics: evidence for a phone-camera lab

Researched 2026-11-28 using PubMed plus web sources. Every citation below was retrieved and checked in this session (PMID or DOI given). A number with no citation is marked **unverified**. Values are male elite or high-performance unless stated otherwise.

**Read first: four caveats that limit how far any of this goes**

1. **Most links are correlations, not causes.** Many samples are small (n = 6 to 50). One systematic review of intrinsic risk factors for lumbar injury in fast bowlers rated 23 of 25 studies as high risk of bias and found the results conflicting (Farhart 2023, PMID 37730648, doi:10.1186/s13102-023-00732-1). Treat every fault threshold as a flag for a coach to review. None of them is a diagnosis.
2. **Speed and safety pull in different directions.** A straighter front knee is linked with faster balls in several studies. The same straighter knee is also linked with higher lumbo-pelvic load (Crewe 2013) and with the lumbar stress fracture group (Portus 2004). The app must not simply reward "straighter is better".
3. **Male thresholds do not transfer to female bowlers.** Female bowlers are more front-on and rotate the pelvis and trunk during the delivery stride, with a different side-flexion strategy (Felton 2019, PMID 30244646, doi:10.1080/02640414.2018.1522700; Jacobs 2024, PMID 38797081, doi:10.1016/j.jbiomech.2024.112157).
4. **No study has validated a smartphone or MediaPipe system for cricket bowling kinematics against 3D motion capture.** All the accuracy figures below come from other movements or from IMUs. Treat them as a best case.

Angle convention used in this file: knee 180° means fully straight. Bowlers are right-arm unless stated, so "front leg" means the left leg.

### Side strain (internal oblique, non-bowling-arm side)
- More than half of 207 first-class fast bowlers in Australia and England had had at least one side strain. 77% of first injuries happened at age 24 or under, and 30% recurred. **The injury was not associated with side-bridge endurance, active lateral-flexion range or height.** It was associated only with the volume of first-class cricket played (Nealon 2018, PMID 28742615, doi:10.1097/JSM.0000000000000450).
- MRI case series: the strain is on the non-bowling-arm side and recovery is long (Humphries & Jamison 2004, Br J Sports Med 38(5):e21; abstract only, not PubMed-verified).
- The proposed mechanism is the front arm pulling down together with trunk side flexion, which is **coaching and clinical opinion**. So: side-plank endurance is a reasonable general prescription, but there is no evidence it prevents side strain. Do not claim it does.

## 2. Action classification (Portus et al. 2004, AIS)

Classification is made **at back-foot contact** from transverse-plane shoulder alignment, plus hip–shoulder separation and shoulder counter-rotation. Thresholds are as reproduced in secondary sources; the original paper (Sports Biomech 3:263–284, PMID 15552585) was not read in full.

| Action | Shoulder alignment at BFC | HSS at BFC | SCR (BFC → min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Side-on | < 210° | < 30° | < 30° |
| Semi-open | 210°–240° | < 30° | < 30° |
| Front-on | > 240° | < 30° | < 30° |
| **Mixed** | any | (≥ 30° in some versions) | **≥ 30°** |

- Mixed actions are the type consistently linked with lumbar injury. There is no evidence of extra risk for front-on or semi-open actions when counter-rotation is low (Elliott 2000; Morton 2014).
- A high share of professional bowlers are mixed under the > 30° criterion (Ranson 2008).
- In juniors, most differences between action types appear at BFC, not FFC (Schaefer 2020).
- Later papers use modified angle conventions, so cut-offs vary between studies.
- **Confidence: moderate.** The numeric thresholds above come from secondary sources (Konstanz ISBS proceedings and Harnett 2022, PMID 35415020) and need checking against the original paper.
- **ECB version:** no separate ECB numeric definition was found. It is **unverified** whether the ECB uses identical cut-offs.
- **Phone implication:** a single side-on camera cannot classify the action, because every criterion is a transverse-plane angle. The app currently lets the user self-report the action, which is the honest approach.

## 3. Elbow-extension law (ICC 15°) and why a phone cannot certify it

- **Rule:** a delivery is illegal if the elbow extends more than 15° between the upper arm reaching horizontal and ball release. Hyperextension is excluded. The limit has applied to all bowlers since November 2004. Testing happens only at an ICC Accredited Testing Centre under the ICC Standard Analysis Protocols (ICC Illegal Bowling Actions page, icc-cricket.com).
- **Why 2D video fails:**
  1. Elbow flexion-extension happens out of the camera plane while the upper arm is rotating, so a 2D projected angle mixes in shoulder rotation and "carry" angle.
  2. Even 3D video-based systems had RMS errors of 2.3° to 9.4° depending on the joint-centre model. Occluding the arm with a shirt raised error to 7.8°–9.0° (Elliott 2007, PMID 17307186, doi:10.1016/j.jbiomech.2006.12.012). That error is the same size as the decision margin.
  3. 2D overhead shoulder alignment had mean random errors of 9.5° at BFC, 11.7° at FFC and **22.5° at release** (Elliott 2002, PMID 12137180, doi:10.1080/02640410252925170).
  4. The arm moves fastest around release, which is where 30 or 60 fps phone video is coarsest.
  5. Elbow extension angular velocity at release separated suspect bowlers better than the 15° angle did (Ferdinands 2007).
  6. A bowler with a fixed elbow flexion can add wrist speed through upper-arm internal rotation without breaking the law (Marshall 2003, PMID 14658246, doi:10.1080/14763140308522808). An apparently bent arm is therefore not evidence of throwing.
- **App rule:** never output "legal" or "illegal". At most, say "the arm looks bent in this clip; only an accredited 3D test can judge legality".

- Smart-ball gyroscope work measures spin, torque and precession, not body kinematics (Fuss/Ferdinands 2021–2024, e.g. PMID 34696156, doi:10.3390/s21206942).

- **Bat-speed determinants**, summarised: separation at the start of the downswing, lead-elbow extension and wrist uncocking (Peploe 2019). Bat speed at impact plus impact location explained 68% of post-impact ball speed.
- **Hip–shoulder sequencing in the cricket drive specifically: unverified.** The proximal-to-distal sequencing evidence found here is mostly from baseball and softball. The front upper limb in a cricket drive works almost as one unit, with segment peak speeds nearly simultaneous before impact (Stuelcken 2005), which is **not** a classic proximal-to-distal sequence.

**Cricket-specific validation that exists:**
- IMU spinal kinematics and tibial accelerations vs optoelectronic capture in 35 elite bowlers: r > 0.8, ICC > 0.7 (Senington 2021, PMID 34173966, doi:10.1007/s11517-021-02381-3).

## 7. Notes for the app (docs/athlete-combine.md "Bowling front knee")

- The current rule (≥ +10° = extender, ≤ −10° = flexor, else brace) does not cover Portus's **flexor-extender** category (≥ 10° flexion **and then** ≥ 10° extension). Classifying from net change alone mislabels a flexor-extender as a brace or an extender. Use the minimum knee angle between FFC and BR, not only the two endpoints.
- Any "extender = good" message needs the counterweight that a more extended front knee went with higher lumbo-pelvic load (Crewe 2013). Present it as a speed trait, not a safety trait.
- Female bowlers need their own interpretation (see caveat 3 at the top).
- Exercise prescriptions above that are marked "coaching practice" must not be described as evidence-based in any public-facing copy. That copy should go through `compliance-checker` (no claims that an exercise prevents injury).
